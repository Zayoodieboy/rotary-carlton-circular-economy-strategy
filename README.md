## Part A - Problem Description: Textile Waste

Textile waste is a severe problem resulted in a significant negative environmental impact in Australia (M.A. Bukhari, 2018). Out of 100 million garment products, a staggering 92 million tons of textile waste are generated annually (Igina, 2022). Only 15% of these clothes are recycled, while the remainder ends up in landfills (Kelly, 2022). Textile waste takes up to 200 years to decompose due to its high fiber content of up to 69% (Style, 2022). The fashion industry now ranks as the fourth largest environmental polluter globally with its intensive use of energy, water, and chemicals (J. Riba, 2020).

### Causes

The fashion industry's root causes in Melbourne include fast fashion (excessive textile production), International migration (overconsumption, low 3Rs rate) (Jr, 2023). The fast fashion business model creates a constant desire for new collections, leading customers to feel outdated and buy more (SAXON, 2022). Retail giants like ZARA, and H&M, use this strategy to attract young customers and generate media attention (Barnes, 2006). In 2022, overseas migration in Australia increases by 171,000 (Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2022). Clothing consumption has also skyrocketed, with 80 billion garments purchased each year, a 400% increase compared to previous decades (Morgan, 2015).

### Effects

The main effect of textile waste is water, microfiber, air, and soil pollution. Toxic substances, including mercury and arsenic, are dumped into rivers, threatening human health (Noyes, 2022). Cotton cultivation further exacerbates water scarcity, with 9,700 liters needed for every kilogram of cotton (Leahy, 2015). 35% of microplastic pollution comes from fashion, and these microfibers can enter the food chain, leading to diseases (Milton, 2023). Manufacturing and transporting textiles require substantial energy, and synthetic fibers from fossil fuels intensify this energy consumption. Practices like overgrazing cashmere goats, raising sheep for wool, and using chemicals in cotton cultivation lead to soil degradation (Charpail, 2022).

## Part B - Analysis of the External Business Environment

### PESTEL Analysis

The PESTLE model provides RCC's clothes recycling project with a comprehensive framework for analysing the macro-environmental factors, including political, economic, social, technological, legal, and environmental aspects, assisting in making well-informed strategic decisions (Achinas et al., 2019).

* **P:** The Australian government provides a stable external environment for RCC's project by funding relative campaign, persistent environmental-friendly policy, deductible tax for charity and innovation encouragement in clothing industry (Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water, 2022; Australian Taxation Office, 2018; Australian Fashion Council, 2023).
* **E:** Economic factors like current and expected economic growth, relatively stable overall economic demand, stable low unemployment rates, and increased disposable income for consumers and businesses all contribute to the successful implementation of the project. However, RCC should prepare strategies for economic uncertainties (Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS), 2023; Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA), 2023).
* **S:** Social factors such as diverse population, environmental awareness, sociocultural changes, and improved living standards support the project, but RCC must accommodate various clothing needs and improve customer service to stay competitive (ABS, 2016, 2021, 2023; RBA, 2023; AUSTRALIAN FASHION COUNCIL, 2023; Rotary Club of Carlton (RCC), 2023).
* **T:** Technological advancements in digital technology, automation, R&D, distribution, manufacturing, and logistics benefit the project. RCC should monitor promising innovations and integrate them into second-hand clothing recycling and reuse (Forbes, 2022; McKinsey, 2023).
* **L:** Legally, Competition Law, Health and Safety Law, App & website launching requirements needs to be obeyed by RCC's project at any time (Australian Competition and Consumer Commission, 2022; Government of Victoria, 2017; Australian Government, 2019).
* **E:** Environmentally, focusing on aspects such as carbon footprint, recycling procedures and waste management, and sustainability will help RCC achieve good performance in environmental protection and sustainable development, creating a green and eco-friendly brand image for the project (Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water, 2022; Sarkar, 2019).

### Porter's Five Forces Model

According to Scott (2023), when a non-profit organisation like the Rotary Club of Carlton uses the Porter's Five Forces model, they can assess the negotiating power of its suppliers, evaluate the threat of new competitors entering the market, and develop strategies to differentiate its partner and improve its competitive position in the market.

## Part C - Competitor Analysis

The following competitive analysis provides an examination of the current not for profit landscape and the Rotary Club of Carlton's standing within this environment. This analysis was conducted focusing on the three key competitors outlined by the RCC during in class consultations, these competitors are: the Lions Club, Kiwanis Club and the Freemasons, who each have both a national and localised presence. This analysis will consider the objectives, membership structure and key project involvements for each competitor as well as the RCC.

### Lions Club
* **Objectives:** Primarily focuses on sight preservation, disaster relief, and community development projects.
* **Membership Structure:** Volunteer-driven organisation, with clubs worldwide attracting community members from various backgrounds.
* **Key Projects:** SightFirst program, Lions Quest, and Disaster Preparedness & Relief.

### Kiwanis Club
* **Objectives:** Primary mission is to serve the children of the world, with projects in education, health, and community development.
* **Membership Structure:** Open to community members.
* **Key Projects:** Eliminate Project (eliminating maternal and neonatal tetanus), Key Club, and Builders Club.

### Freemasons
* **Objectives:** Fraternal organisation with a focus on personal development, charitable giving, and community service.
* **Membership Structure:** Membership-based organisation, with a hierarchical structure and numerous lodges worldwide.
* **Key Projects:** Masonic Charitable Foundation, local charity support, and educational programs.

### Rotary Club of Carlton
* **Objectives:** The Rotary Club addresses diverse social, environmental, and humanitarian issues.
* **Membership Structure:** Volunteer-driven organization with members from various professional backgrounds, fostering networking and collaboration.
* **Key Projects:** Polio eradication, peacebuilding and conflict resolution, and clean water and sanitation projects.

### Literature Review

RCC would benefit from adopting ecosystem growth strategies to scale its social impact. Ecosystem growth can reach a larger scale beyond organizational boundaries by dealing with pressing global issues through developing or supporting a supportive social enterprise ecosystem (Thompson, 2018; Montgomery et al., 2012). An ecosystem defines a set of features that collectively create a supportive and facilitative climate for a business to succeed and blossom (Spigel & Harrison, 2018; Elsayed, 2018). Among such features, coalition work plays a major role in a social enterprise ecosystem (Montgomery et al., 2012). RCC can build a coalition with others to promote a circular economy. Partnership with other organizations is a key strategy for social enterprises to access resources and increase impact (Cantele et al., 2020; Austin & Seitanidi, 2012).

## Conclusion

The necessity of promoting a circular economy can be chiefly attributed to clothing waste as discussed in part A of the report, where most waste is not being sent to landfill arguably caused by fast fashion and population growth, the effects of this have been shown to pollute the air, water, soil and microfiber. Our solution is to fundraise for the RCC while raising public awareness and promoting recycling among our partners. The returns from fundraising will be made to local businesses in the fashion industry to encourage a circular economy. Our external analysis has shown that favourable conditions for these recommendations exist in the political & social landscapes (due to special tax treatment & funding opportunities for social enterprises, and the growing social movement towards environmentally friendly attitudes). However, the high threat of substitutes in the clothing market may hinder the effectiveness of a second-hand clothing initiative. Rivalry amongst similar organisations can also be seen as high. RCC possesses strengths that will assist in the implementation of our recommendations such as already possessing a strong talent pool within the club, as well as strong connections which may be able to help facilitate marketing efforts. While the availability of volunteers may cause an issue to the financial feasibility of our business ideas, they are overall both suitable for RCC, acceptable to key stakeholders and financially feasible if the cost of such ideas is justified by the level of impact within the community.

### Workshop Further Explanation

Hold workshops to educate and engage the benefits of a circular economy and sustainable consumption practices. The fashion industry is a significant contributor to environmental pollution and waste, and promoting sustainable practices such as repurposing and up-cycling can reduce its negative impact. Interactive workshops and education can increase awareness and engagement in sustainability initiatives, and measuring the impact of sustainable fashion initiatives is essential. According to research by Wilson, Velasco, and Winter (2019), promoting sustainable consumption practices can lead to significant reductions in environmental impact. The study emphasizes that educating consumers about the benefits of sustainable consumption practices can lead to long-term behaviour change and contribute to the transition towards a more sustainable society.

The use of themed workshops as a tool for increasing sustainability is backed by multiple studies. For instance, a 2019 study by Nilsson and Berglund discovered that themed workshops on food waste were successful in lowering home waste. Huber et al.'s (2017) further research found that themed workshops on zero waste were effective at encouraging behavioural change and lowering waste in the workplace. Likewise, it has been discovered that workshops on sustainable fashion are successful in encouraging more conscientious consumer behaviour (Moore et al., 2018).
