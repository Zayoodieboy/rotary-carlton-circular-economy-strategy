## Part A - Problem Description: Textile Waste

Textile waste is a severe problem resulting in a significant negative environmental impact in Australia (M.A. Bukhari, 2018)[cite: 75]. Out of 100 million garment products, a staggering 92 million tons of textile waste are generated annually (Igina, 2022)[cite: 75]. Only 15% of these clothes are recycled, while the remainder ends up in landfills (Kelly, 2022)[cite: 75]. Textile waste takes up to 200 years to decompose due to its high fiber content of up to 69% (Style, 2022)[cite: 75]. The fashion industry now ranks as the fourth largest environmental polluter globally with its intensive use of energy, water, and chemicals (J. Riba, 2020)[cite: 75].

### Causes & Problem Tree

The fashion industry's root causes in Melbourne include fast fashion (excessive textile production), International migration (overconsumption, low 3Rs rate) (Jr, 2023)[cite: 75]. The fast fashion business model creates a constant desire for new collections, leading customers to feel outdated and buy more (Saxon, 2022)[cite: 75]. Retail giants like ZARA, and H&M, use this strategy to attract young customers and generate media attention (Barnes, 2006)[cite: 75]. In 2022, overseas migration in Australia increases by 171,000 (Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2022)[cite: 75]. Clothing consumption has also skyrocketed, with 80 billion garments purchased each year, a 400% increase compared to previous decades (Morgan, 2015)[cite: 76].

> **[📊 Figure 1 - Problem Tree of Textile Waste]**  
> *(Visually maps root causes: Fast Fashion / Excessive Textile Production & Migration / Overconsumption leading down to Problem: Textile Waste, branching upward into consequences: Air/CO2, Global Warming, Soil Deforestation, Microfiber/Disease, and Water/Scarcity of Fresh Water)*[cite: 76]

### Effects & Fishbone Diagram

The main effect of textile waste is water, microfiber, air, and soil pollution[cite: 76]. Toxic substances, including mercury and arsenic, are dumped into rivers, threatening human health (Noyes, 2022)[cite: 76]. Cotton cultivation further exacerbates water scarcity, with 9,700 liters needed for every kilogram of cotton (Leahy, 2015)[cite: 76]. 35% of microplastic pollution comes from fashion, and these microfibers can enter the food chain, leading to diseases (Milton, 2023)[cite: 76]. Manufacturing and transporting textiles require substantial energy, and synthetic fibers from fossil fuels intensify this energy consumption[cite: 76]. Practices like overgrazing cashmere goats, raising sheep for wool, and using chemicals in cotton cultivation lead to soil degradation (Charpail, 2022)[cite: 76].

> *(Categorizes environmental effects into Microfibers entering the food chain, Water toxic substances & scarcity, Air CO2 production from manufacturing/transport, and Soil degradation from chemical use)*[cite: 77]

## Part B - Analysis of the External Business Environment

### PESTEL Analysis
The PESTLE model provides RCC's clothes recycling project with a comprehensive framework for analysing the macro-environmental factors, including political, economic, social, technological, legal, and environmental aspects, assisting in making well-informed strategic decisions (Achinas et al., 2019)[cite: 79].

* **Political (P):** The Australian government provides a stable external environment for RCC's project by funding relative campaigns, persistent environmentally-friendly policies, deductible tax for charity, and innovation encouragement in the clothing industry (Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water, 2022; Australian Taxation Office, 2018; Australian Fashion Council, 2023)[cite: 79].
* **Economic (E):** Economic factors like current and expected economic growth, relatively stable overall economic demand, stable low unemployment rates, and increased disposable income for consumers and businesses all contribute to the successful implementation of the project[cite: 79]. However, RCC should prepare strategies for economic uncertainties (Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS), 2023; Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA), 2023)[cite: 79].
* **Social (S):** Social factors such as a diverse population, environmental awareness, sociocultural changes, and improved living standards support the project, but RCC must accommodate various clothing needs and improve customer service to stay competitive (ABS, 2016, 2021, 2023; RBA, 2023; Australian Fashion Council, 2023; Rotary Club of Carlton (RCC), 2023)[cite: 79].
* **Technological (T):** Technological advancements in digital technology, automation, R&D, distribution, manufacturing, and logistics benefit the project[cite: 79]. RCC should monitor promising innovations and integrate them into second-hand clothing recycling and reuse (Forbes, 2022; McKinsey, 2023)[cite: 79].
* **Legal (L):** Legally, Competition Law, Health and Safety Law, and App & website launching requirements need to be obeyed by RCC's project at any time (Australian Competition and Consumer Commission, 2022; Government of Victoria, 2017; Australian Government, 2019)[cite: 79].
* **Environmental (E):** Environmentally, focusing on aspects such as carbon footprint, recycling procedures and waste management, and sustainability will help RCC achieve good performance in environmental protection and sustainable development, creating a green and eco-friendly brand image for the project (Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water, 2022; Sarkar, 2019)[cite: 79, 80].
